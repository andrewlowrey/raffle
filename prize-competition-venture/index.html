Prize Competition Venture — Business Case v0.4

Status: Draft for founder review — not yet approved, not yet legally or financially validated Phase: 0.5 (Business Case) — precedes 01 (Business & Compliance Model) Supersedes: v0.1 (initial draft), v0.2 (review response), v0.3 (regulatory analysis) — consolidates all three. Capital requirement is derived from the cash flow model in §6, not assumed as an input.

Executive summary

Verdict: conditional go — but not at the pricing and competition sequence currently modelled.

The market is real and the strategy is defensible. The blocker is unit economics, and it is fixable but not yet fixed.

The opportunity

The UK prize competition sector is worth approximately £1.3bn annually, with 7.4m adult participants and 400+ operators (DCMS research). It is established, growing and fragmented — no operator owns the full customer lifecycle. Competitor analysis across Omaze, That Prize Guy, BOTB, Elite, 7 Days Performance and Dream Car Giveaways found each strong in one area and weak in others, leaving a genuine gap for a combined proposition.

Strategy: premium automotive positioning ("the premium prize platform for people who actually love cars"), supported by cash and instant-win products, differentiated on trust, transparency and disciplined competition economics. Operated on a hybrid agent-human model — AI agents do the work across every function; humans approve wherever regulation, money or customer wellbeing is involved.

The finding that changes everything 🔴

HMRC confirmed in February 2026 that prize draws offering both paid and free entry routes are not VAT-exempt. Paid entries carry VAT at 20%. HMRC began writing to operators directly in July 2026.

Applied to the planned five-competition launch sequence:

Pre-VAT With 20% VAT
Cumulative contribution over 5 competitions -£16,846 -£44,434
Competitions that can break even 5 of 5 2 of 5
Capital required (base case) ~£60k ~£99k
Capital required (downside) ~£82k ~£122k

Three of the five planned competitions cannot break even at any sell-through — break-even lands at 102-105%. This is a structural pricing failure, not a sensitivity. More capital does not fix it.

There is an unavoidable tension at the heart of the model: the free entry route is what keeps the business outside gambling licensing, and it is precisely what disqualifies it from VAT exemption. Both cannot be optimised.

The second material finding 🔴

There is no licensed fallback. Lottery operating licences in Great Britain are available only to non-commercial societies and External Lottery Managers acting for them, with a minimum 20% of proceeds to a good cause. There is no licensed route to a commercial for-profit prize competition business.

If the free entry route fails the statutory test in Schedule 2 of the Gambling Act 2005, the business model is unlawful with nowhere to go — and promoting an unlicensed lottery is a criminal offence, not a regulatory breach. This raises the stakes on getting the entry mechanism legally verified before build, not after.

Capital requirement

£115k-£142k all-in, derived bottom-up from the competition cash flow model — not assumed. Range driven by sell-through sensitivity and the VAT position. Excludes founder living costs.

Three blocking items

Nothing else should proceed until these are resolved:

  1. Rebuild pricing and competition sequence for 20% VAT — five options set out in §6.8; one must be chosen and modelled
  2. Specialist VAT advice — confirm HMRC's position, input recovery by prize category, and whether competitions 1-3 can run below the £90k registration threshold
  3. Legal opinion on the free entry mechanism — convenience parity under Sch. 2 para. 8(1)(b), and the instant-win reveal path under Sch. 2 para. 6

Revised risk order

VAT/unit economics is now risk #1, ahead of customer acquisition. Free entry route execution is #2. Technology is #9 — the only well-understood risk in the list.

How to read this document

  • 🔴 ASSUMED — no external validation yet, needs real-world input before it can be trusted
  • 🟡 MODELLED — derived from other assumptions through calculation; only as good as its inputs

1. Founding situation 🟢 DECIDED

Hybrid agent-human operating model, founder-accountable. AI agents do the work — research, drafting, analysis, execution, monitoring — across every function including the regulated and relationship-led ones. What varies is not whether an agent is involved, but how much authority it has to act without a human. Nothing is purely human-operated; nothing sensitive is fully autonomous.

Why the modes matter, rather than a human/agent split. Two constraints shape where the gates go — but neither means "no agent involvement":

  1. Regulatory accountability. The DCMS Code expects identifiable human responsibility for complaints and responsible-participation intervention. That's satisfied by Mode B — the agent can do the detection, scoring, evidence assembly and recommendation, provided a named human makes and owns the decision. Fully autonomous harm decisions would not be defensible; agent-prepared ones are.
  2. Trust-critical and relationship work. Winner verification and supplier/acquirer negotiation still get heavy agent support (verification checks, comms drafts, price benchmarking, underwriting packs) — the human is the approver and counterparty, not the researcher.

Mode C is where agents cut external cost. Legal, accounting and procurement remain human-led, but agent preparation should reduce billable hours and improve negotiating position — e.g. arriving at a solicitor with drafted terms to review rather than a blank brief, or at a dealer with benchmarked pricing. The £3k-£7.5k legal provision in §8 assumes agent-prepared inputs.

The honest tradeoff (🔴 ASSUMED, needs validating in build): this model reduces headcount cost but increases platform build scope. Approval queues, audit trails, guardrails and escalation routing are all real build work. Cost shifts from salaries to build time and complexity — it doesn't disappear. §12 carries this as an explicit scope line.

Single-human risk: the founder is currently the only approver for every Mode B and Mode C gate. Agents reduce the volume of work but not the approval bottleneck. Named risk in §18, open item #7.

Working launch horizon: ~3-4 months after legal and payment approval — not a year-long build.

Market context: UK prize draw/competition sector ≈ £1.3bn annually, ~7.4m adult participants, 400+ operators (DCMS). Large enough to justify testing; fragmented enough that no single operator owns the category.

3. Prize funding model 🟢 DECIDED / 🔴 ASSUMED

Decided: Cash prizes + supplier-backed physical prizes. For vehicles, pursue dealer relationships enabling deferred settlement, trade pricing, and marketing/photography access rather than paying full retail upfront. Prize indemnity insurance is explicitly rejected for guaranteed random-draw competitions — the risk profile doesn't fit (the prize is always awarded, not contingent on a low-probability outcome). Insurance is instead considered separately for prize loss/damage/theft, not payout indemnity (see §11).

Decided — revenue recycling policy: Competition 1 does not fund itself. Initial capital funds Competition 1. Every competition after that draws on:

Available Capital = Working Capital + Released Competition Revenue

"Released" is the operative word — see §7. Revenue sitting in an acquirer rolling reserve is not available capital.

Decided — undersell policy: If a competition underperforms, the next competition's prize/marketing exposure is reduced. Capital discipline is a business rule enforced by policy (and eventually the platform), not solved by hoping the next competition performs better.

🔴 Open item — needs a real number, not a flowchart:

If net capital draw on a competition exceeds £[X], cap the next competition's prize exposure at £[Y]% of remaining available capital.

Based on the modelling in §6, a reasonable starting point is X = £10,000 (roughly the worst single-competition draw in the base case) and Y = 50% of remaining available capital. This should be treated as a first draft, not a final rule — revisit once real competition 1 data exists.

6.8 What must change before this business is viable 🔴 OPEN

The VAT finding forces at least one of the following. None is optional:

  1. Raise realised entry yield by ~20%+. Restores the pre-VAT position arithmetically, but is an untested demand assumption in a price-sensitive market where competitors advertise 9p-99p entries.
  2. Cut the marketing cost floor. The reason small competitions can't break even is that marketing/creative costs don't scale down with prize size. Needs organic/referral-led acquisition, which contradicts the paid-social plan in §13.
  3. Drop small cash competitions entirely. Start at the £25k+ car level where break-even is at least achievable. Increases capital at risk on competition 1 — directly against the "start small" logic in §5.
  4. Improve prize economics via procurement. The §14 dealer relationship (£40k retail at £34k) becomes essential rather than advantageous — it is now load-bearing.
  5. Register for VAT deliberately and structure for input recovery. Where prizes carry recoverable VAT, the net position improves. Cash prizes never will.

VAT registration threshold consideration: the threshold is £90k taxable turnover on a rolling 12-month basis. Competitions 1-3 total ~£72k gross — below it. Competition 4 crosses it. Staying under the threshold avoids VAT entirely but caps the business at roughly hobby scale and is incompatible with the §15 targets (£250k by month 6, £1m annualised). It is not a strategy, but it does mean competitions 1-3 may be VAT-free in practice, which is worth modelling as a separate scenario before dismissing.

This is now the single most important open item in the document — ahead of the acquirer question. The §5 competition sequence and §6 pricing both need rebuilding around it, and that should happen before Phase 1 spec work rather than after.

8. Regulatory & licensing model — GB (England, Wales, Scotland) 🟢 DECIDED / 🔴 ASSUMED

Scope note: this analysis covers Great Britain only. The Gambling Act 2005 applies to England, Wales and Scotland. Northern Ireland operates under separate legislation and is out of scope for launch. Ireland is out of scope entirely.

8.1 The question that defines the entire business

There is one binary legal question, and everything else follows from it:

Is what we are running a lottery?

If yes, it is almost certainly an illegal lottery, because lottery operating licences are not available to commercial for-profit operators (see §8.5). If no, it sits outside gambling regulation and can be run commercially for profit.

Per s.14 Gambling Act 2005, an arrangement is a simple lottery where all three of the following are present:

1. Persons are REQUIRED TO PAY to participate
2. One or more prizes are allocated to members of a class
3. Prizes are allocated by a process relying WHOLLY ON CHANCE

A complex lottery (s.14(3)) is the same but with a series of processes, the first of which relies wholly on chance — this catches multi-stage draws.

Elements 2 and 3 are unavoidable in our product: we allocate prizes, and we do it randomly. The entire legal structure therefore rests on defeating element 1 — the requirement to pay. That is the whole ballgame.

8.2 The four routes available, and which are actually viable

Route Mechanism Licensable? Viable for us?
A — Free entry route Paid entry + genuine free alternative (Sch. 2 para. 8) No licence needed ✅ Recommended
B — Genuine skill Skill/judgement/knowledge defeats "wholly on chance" (s.14(5)) No licence needed ⚠️ Weak — high risk
C — Society lottery / ELM Licensed lottery for good causes Licence/registration ❌ Not available commercially
D — Free-to-enter only No payment at all No licence needed ❌ No revenue model

8.3 Route A — the free entry route (RECOMMENDED) 🟢 DECIDED

Schedule 2, paragraph 8 sets out four conditions. All four must be satisfied. Failing any one of them means participants are treated as required to pay — and the arrangement becomes an unlicensed lottery.

# Condition Platform implication
1 Every eligible individual has a genuine choice between paying and sending a communication Free route must be available to everyone, always — not a hidden fallback
2 The communication may be ordinary post (first/second class, no special delivery), or another method that is neither more expensive nor less convenient than paying "Not less convenient" is the hard test. A 6-step postal form against 2-click Apple Pay is legally fragile
3 The choice is publicised so as to be likely to come to the attention of each person proposing to participate Prominent, pre-purchase, on the competition page — not buried in T&Cs
4 The prize allocation system does not differentiate between paid and free entrants One Entry Engine, identical draw treatment — validates the Stage 1 §16 architecture decision

Condition 4 is the architectural one. It is why paid, postal and free entries must share a single Entry Engine with identical eligibility and draw logic — this is now a legal requirement, not a design preference.

Condition 2 is the operational one, and the most commonly failed. The DCMS Code reinforces it: where a free route doesn't offer a genuine choice, that strongly indicates participants are in practice required to pay (Code clause 2.4). The ASA takes the same position — a free route cannot carry any charge above the normal cost of the communication method (ASA guidance on free entry routes).

8.4 Route B — the skill test (REJECTED as primary model) 🟢 DECIDED

s.14(5) provides that a skill/judgement/knowledge requirement is still treated as wholly chance unless it can reasonably be expected to:

  • (a) prevent a significant proportion of participants from receiving a prize, and
  • (b) prevent a significant proportion of people who wish to participate from doing so

Both limbs must be satisfied. This is a demanding test — and note the commercial paradox: to pass, the question must be hard enough to deter a significant proportion of your own customers. A question genuinely difficult enough to satisfy s.14(5) is directly hostile to conversion.

BOTB's Spot the Ball model (expert-judged coordinate selection) is a genuine attempt at this. A multiple-choice question of the "What colour is the sky?" variety is not, and the Gambling Commission has been consistent that trivial questions don't satisfy the test.

Decision: do not rely on skill as the legal basis. It may be layered in later as a product feature, but the free entry route remains the legal foundation.

8.5 Route C — why a UKGC licence is NOT an alternative 🟢 DECIDED

This deserves stating plainly, because "just get licensed instead" is the intuitive assumption and it is wrong.

Lottery operating licences are issued only to non-commercial societies and External Lottery Managers acting on their behalf. Per Gambling Commission guidance, lotteries cannot be run for private or commercial gain, and a minimum 20% of proceeds must go to the good cause. Small society lotteries are capped at £20,000 per draw / £250,000 per calendar year and require local authority registration.

Implications:

  • There is no licensed route to a commercial for-profit prize competition business in GB
  • Becoming an ELM for a charity partner is possible, but caps you at ≥20% of proceeds to the cause and fundamentally changes the business model
  • "Applying for a licence" is not a fallback if the free-entry structure fails. There is no plan B of that kind — which is precisely why the free entry route must be executed correctly from day one

This materially raises the stakes on §8.3. Getting it wrong doesn't mean a fine and a licence application; it means the business model is unlawful.

8.6 Schedule 2 traps that directly constrain product design 🔴 CRITICAL

Schedule 2 contains provisions that bite on specific product mechanics from the Stage 2 competitor work:

Provision What it says Product constraint
Para. 2(c) Paying for goods/services at a price reflecting the opportunity to participate counts as paying Cannot dodge the rules by bundling entries with merchandise
Para. 6 A requirement to pay to discover whether a prize has been won counts as paying to participate Instant-win reveal cannot be paywalled. The Stage 2 "reveal experience" must never charge to unlock a result
Para. 7 A requirement to pay to take possession of a prize counts as paying to participate No delivery fees, admin fees or claim charges on prizes
Para. 3 Immaterial who receives the payment or benefit Cannot route payment through a third party to break the chain
Para. 5 Normal-rate communication costs don't count as payment; "normal rate" must not reflect the entry opportunity No premium-rate numbers or surcharged entry channels

Paragraph 6 is the one most likely to be tripped by accident, given instant wins are core to the MVP (§12). Any design where the customer pays to reveal, unlock, or check a result — rather than paying to enter — risks converting the whole product into a lottery.

Paragraph 7 means the "free delivery" promise in the Stage 2 winner SLA is a legal requirement, not a customer-service nicety.

8.8 Consequences of getting it wrong 🔴 HIGH IMPACT

Promoting an unlicensed lottery is a criminal offence, not a regulatory breach. The Gambling Commission has powers to investigate and prosecute illegal gambling and has historically stated it monitors the lottery/prize-competition boundary.

Realistic exposure: criminal liability for the promoter, business shutdown, payment acquirer termination (immediate, and effectively terminal per §7), reputational destruction, and personal liability for the founder as a director. There is no "pay a fine and continue" version of this outcome.

9.1 HMRC / VAT — the most material finding in this document 🔴 CRITICAL

This changes the business model economics fundamentally and was not accounted for in v0.1 or v0.2.

On 17 February 2026, the Exchequer Secretary to the Treasury confirmed in a written parliamentary answer that prize draws offering both paid and free entry routes are not eligible for VAT exemption, and paid entries are subject to VAT at the standard 20% rate.

In July 2026 HMRC launched a one-to-many campaign, writing directly to prize draw operators to press the point (ICAEW, CIOT). Key features:

  • Operators finding an error must correct it via their next VAT return or disclose to HMRC
  • If HMRC finds an undisclosed error later, disclosure is treated as "prompted", increasing penalties
  • Retrospective exposure applies to prior years' revenue
  • Industry estimates suggest margin compression of roughly 25-30% (iGaming Business)

The bitter irony worth naming: the free entry route is what keeps the business outside gambling licensing (§8.3) — and it is also precisely what disqualifies it from VAT exemption. The two positions are in direct tension, and you cannot optimise for both.

Status: 🔴 ASSUMED that HMRC's position holds. The sector is contesting it and litigation is plausible, but planning must assume HMRC is right. Assuming otherwise is not a strategy.

Financial impact is modelled in §6.7 — it is severe.

9.2 CMA / consumer protection — DMCCA 2024 🟠 HIGH

The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 gave the CMA direct enforcement powers from 6 April 2025 — it no longer needs to go to court first. Penalties reach the higher of £300,000 or 10% of global turnover, and the CMA can order consumer redress.

Directly relevant exposures for this product:

Practice Risk
Drip pricing — fees appearing late in checkout Explicitly targeted by CMA enforcement
Urgency/scarcity claims — "only 500 left", countdowns Must be true and verifiable, not merchandising theatre
Fake or incentivised reviews Banned practice
Sell-through percentages If displayed, must be accurate — this is a consumer law claim, not decoration
Subscription auto-renewal (deferred to v2) New DMCCA requirements apply when built

Design consequence: the Stage 2 merchandising engine — badges, countdowns, "% sold", "ending tonight" — sits squarely inside CMA scope. Every urgency signal must be generated from real data with an audit trail. Fabricated urgency is a 10%-of-turnover risk, and the agent-driven merchandising in §1/§10 must be constrained accordingly.

9.3 ASA / advertising — CAP & BCAP 🟠 HIGH

The DCMS Code explicitly does not displace ASA obligations — the ASA continues to assess ads independently and may find a breach even where an operator believes it has met the Code (Code, Context section).

Key requirements:

  • Free entry route must be clear in advertising, not just on site (ASA free entry route guidance)
  • Must not imply participation solves financial problems, is an escape, or provides financial security (Code 1.11; ASA lotteries guidance)
  • Must not exploit young or vulnerable people
  • Advertised prizes must be awarded, or a reasonable equivalent of similar nature and value (ASA high value prize promotions)
  • No targeting under-18s

This is why marketing claims need legal sign-off before spend starts (§8.9), not before checkout ships.

9.4 ICO / data protection 🟡 MEDIUM

UK GDPR and DPA 2018 apply in full. Relevant: consent for marketing, lawful basis for behavioural profiling (the Stage 2 personalisation ambition), winner publicity consent, data retention on entry records vs. audit obligations, and DSAR handling. The winner-publicity consent flow in the Stage 2 fulfilment workflow is a data protection matter, not just a content one.

10. Operating model — agent-first with escalation triggers 🟢 DECIDED

Largest actual business risk — larger than the technology. 400+ existing operators means "better website → customers arrive" does not hold.

Model: Paid social + organic social + influencer/creator + (later) referral + CRM retention. Not SEO-led initially.

  • Journey C makes the para. 6 boundary explicit. A requirement to pay to discover whether a prize has been won counts as paying to participate. Any "unlock your result" pattern would convert the product into an unlicensed lottery. Prototyping it proves the boundary is understood and designed around.
  • Journey A surfaces the pricing problem rather than hiding it. Entry price is set at 79p against a £48,000 prize and 37,500 maximum entries — deliberately the level §6.7 shows requires ~76% sell-through to break even once 20% VAT applies.

Deliberate connection between A and B

Journey A's competition detail screen links directly into Journey B. This is not a prototype convenience — Sch. 2 para. 8(1)(c) requires the free route to be publicised so as to be likely to come to the attention of everyone proposing to participate. The routes must connect pre-purchase, which is why the link sits above the paid call to action rather than in the footer.

What the prototypes do not cover

Account management, wallet, winner fulfilment, admin and agent approval queues (§10, §12), postal intake operations, and any personalisation. Deferred deliberately — these three journeys were chosen to de-risk the blocking items, not to illustrate the full MVP.
